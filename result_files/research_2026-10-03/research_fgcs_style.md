# How accepted FGCS papers write: abstracts, highlights, structure, result language, figures, statistics

Date: 2026-10-03. Scope: Future Generation Computer Systems (Elsevier, ISSN 0167-739X), volumes 126–187 (2021–2026).

## Summary of conventions to copy

1. Abstract: one paragraph, 180–230 words, 8–11 sentences, in fixed order: context (1–2), gap opened with "However", "This paper presents / We present X", design in one enumerated sentence "(i) ... (ii) ... (iii)", scope of evaluation (what, on how many datasets, benchmarks, models), two result sentences with the headline numbers written as "X% higher", "N×", "from A to B", then one sentence of consequence for the practitioner. The winner and the loser of a comparison are named in the same sentence.
2. Highlights: 4–5 bullets, aim at 85 characters: one problem statement, two or three "what we did" statements, one or two outcome statements carrying a number, optionally an artefact bullet.
3. Introduction: context with one striking number; "However" gap paragraph; one-sentence aim; proposal paragraph; "The main contributions of this paper are as follows:" with 3–5 numbered items, each item ending with its headline number; optional named questions or hypotheses (Nikitin "Issue 1–6", Mosaic "E1–E5", Huang "RQ1–RQ3") that are answered by named results subsections; closing "The remainder of this paper is organized as follows." paragraph.
4. Structure: Introduction; Related work (or Background); Problem statement or Methodology; the systems compared; Experimental setup as the first subsection of Experiments (testbed, models, baselines, datasets and metrics, runs); Results split by comparison or by hypothesis, each subsection opening with its finding ("Observation N:"); Ablation as one named subsection inside Results; Discussion (limitations as a paragraph with run-in header); Conclusion with future work; Code and data availability; CRediT. No "Threats to validity" section was seen in FGCS.
5. Results vocabulary: unhedged claim sentence with the number, hedged explanation sentence after it ("This suggests", "may be attributed to", "likely due to"). "Significantly" is paired with a test and p-value by the papers that run tests. Mixed results are stated as such: "no clear winner emerges", "comparable with no statistically significant difference", "The only exception is".
6. Statistics: mean ± std (or standard error) over 3–20 runs stated in the table caption; for LLM stochasticity, repeated prompts with a t-interval 95% CI (Shafee); Wilcoxon or Kruskal–Wallis plus Dunn when many datasets are compared, significance marked by an asterisk in the table; an explicit fairness budget ("The maximum time for evaluation was limited by 10 min for all approaches.").
7. Figures: grouped bar charts and line plots with error bars, one panel per dataset or platform on identical axes, baseline as a dashed line; captions of 20–35 words that say what is plotted, the direction of goodness ("lower is better"), what the error bars are, how many runs, and what each visual encoding means; the reading of the figure stays in the text ("As seen in Figure 4, ...").
8. Conclusion: 250–350 words, four moves: what was built or compared, the findings (numbers optional, most papers restate only the qualitative outcome), one limitations paragraph, one future-work paragraph.

## 2. Abstract anatomy

Numbers from the 15 abstracts I hold in full: 137–318 words, median about 195; 4–13 sentences, typically 8–11; one paragraph, no sub-headings. Abstracts of the 2024–2026 LLM/system papers are longer (190–320 words) than the 2021–2023 ones (140–160).

Sentence roles, in the order they appear in almost every abstract:

1. Context (1–2 sentences). "Intrusion Detection and Prevention Systems (IDS/IPS) in large enterprises can generate hundreds of thousands of alerts per hour" (CyberRAG). "Extensive compute and memory requirements limit the deployment of large language models (LLMs) on any hardware." (Mosaic).
2. Gap, opened with "However" or a negative verb. "However, most of the ML/AI frameworks used nowadays are not fully prepared for this revolution." (Kafka-ML). "State-of-the-art pruning is based on coarse-grained methods. They are time-consuming and inherently remove critical model parameters" (Mosaic).
3. What was done, with the artefact named. "We present CyberRAG, a modular agent-based RAG framework". "This paper presents an empirical evaluation of seven leading LLMs". "This paper compares, using a total of 23 public datasets: i) ... ii) ... iii) ..." (Zoppi).
4. Method or design, often an enumerated list inside one sentence: "(i) fine-tuned classifiers specialized by attack family; (ii) tool adapters for enrichment and alerting; and (iii) an iterative retrieval-and-reason loop".
5. Scope of evaluation. "Mosaic is evaluated using a range of performance and quality metrics on multiple hardware platforms, LLMs, and datasets."; "Each model was evaluated across three progressively complex ETL scenarios".
6. Result. Two schools. Numeric: "Mosaic is 7.19× faster in producing models than existing approaches. Mosaic models achieve up to 84.2% lower perplexity and 31.4% higher accuracy"; "achieving over 94% accuracy per class and a final classification accuracy of 94.92%"; "the False Positive Rate (FPR) for evasion attacks reached 97% with a specialised ML classifier model". Qualitative: "The obtained results confirm the correctness and effectiveness of the proposed approach in the comparison with the state-of-the-art competitors and baseline solutions." (Nikitin); "Results show that there is no benefit in building ensembles instead of using a tree-based classifier" (Zoppi); "We found the LLM Deepseek-Coder-33b-Instruct produced the most passing tests followed by GPT-4-Turbo." (LLM4VV). 6 of 15 abstracts carry at least one number.
7. Implication or recommendation, last sentence. "These findings provide practitioners with empirical guidance on model selection for LLM-assisted pipeline development, while identifying current capability boundaries that require human oversight." (Databricks). "The paper concludes that anomaly-based error and intrusion detectors for critical systems should use the old (but gold) tree-based classifiers" (Zoppi). "These results underscore the need for an additional verification component at the early stage of the CTI pipeline" (Shafee).

How the main conclusion is phrased: a plain declarative with the finding as subject or object ("Results show that ...", "The findings reveal that ...", "The paper concludes that ..."), followed by one sentence of consequence for the practitioner. Comparison papers state the winner and the loser in the same sentence ("Claude Opus 4 and GPT-5 mini were the most reliable, requiring zero or one fix across all prompts, while Claude 3.5 Haiku required up to eight fixes on the most complex scenario").

How numbers are given: percentages with one or two decimals when they are headline accuracies ("94.92%"), relative changes as "X% lower/higher", speed-ups as "N×" (multiplication sign, no space in Mosaic, "10x" in Bruneel), counts of what was evaluated ("seven leading LLMs", "23 public datasets", "over 5000 tests", "56 distinct threat models generated by 6 LLM-based threat elicitation tools"). Ranges are "up to".

### 2.1 Complete verbatim abstracts (closest papers)

Zoppi et al. 2024 (comparison study, tabular, 260 words):

"Recent years have seen a growing involvement of researchers and practitioners in crafting Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) that seem to outperform existing machine learning approaches for solving classification problems as anomaly-based error and intrusion detection. Undoubtedly, classifiers may be very diverse among themselves, and choosing one or another is typically due to the specific task and target system. Designing and training the optimal tabular data classifier requires extensive experimentation, sensitivity analyses, big datasets, and domain-specific knowledge that may not be available at will or considered a non-strategical asset by many companies and stakeholders. This paper compares, using a total of 23 public datasets: i) traditional (tree-based, statistical) supervised classifiers, ii) DNNs that are specifically designed for classifying tabular data, iii) DNNs for image classification that are applied to tabular data after converting data points into images, alone and as ensembles. Experimental results and related discussions show clear advantages in adopting tree-based classifiers for anomaly-based error and intrusion detection in tabular data as they outperform their competitors, including DNNs. Then, individual classifiers are compared against ensembles using different combinations of the classifiers considered in this study as base-learners, providing a unified final response through many meta-learning strategies. Results show that there is no benefit in building ensembles instead of using a tree-based classifier as Random Forests, eXtreme Gradient Boosting or Extra Trees. The paper concludes that anomaly-based error and intrusion detectors for critical systems should use the old (but gold) tree-based classifiers, which are also easier to fine-tune, and understand; plus, they require less time and resources to learn their model."

Martina et al. 2026 (empirical LLM evaluation, 167 words):

"Building production-ready data pipelines on enterprise platforms requires expertise spanning distributed computing, data integration, and platform-specific tooling — a process that Large Language Models (LLMs) may help accelerate. This paper presents an empirical evaluation of seven leading LLMs — from the GPT-5, Claude 4, and Qwen3 families — on their ability to generate functional PySpark data pipelines for the Databricks platform. Each model was evaluated across three progressively complex ETL scenarios (data quality validation, temporal aggregation, and multi-source integration) using a fix-count metric measuring iterative LLM-driven error corrections. Results show that Claude Opus 4 and GPT-5 mini were the most reliable, requiring zero or one fix across all prompts, while Claude 3.5 Haiku required up to eight fixes on the most complex scenario. Code style compliance scores (Pylint/PEP8) proved orthogonal to functional correctness, with some models producing fully functional pipelines despite low style scores. These findings provide practitioners with empirical guidance on model selection for LLM-assisted pipeline development, while identifying current capability boundaries that require human oversight."

Blefari et al. 2025, CyberRAG (LLM agent system, 227 words):

"Intrusion Detection and Prevention Systems (IDS/IPS) in large enterprises can generate hundreds of thousands of alerts per hour, overwhelming analysts with logs requiring rapidly evolving expertise. Conventional machine-learning detectors reduce alert volume but still yield many false positives, while standard Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) pipelines often retrieve irrelevant context and fail to justify predictions. We present CyberRAG, a modular agent-based RAG framework that delivers real-time classification, explanation, and structured reporting for cyber-attacks. A central LLM agent orchestrates: (i) fine-tuned classifiers specialized by attack family; (ii) tool adapters for enrichment and alerting; and (iii) an iterative retrieval-and-reason loop that queries a domain-specific knowledge base until evidence is relevant and self-consistent. Unlike traditional RAG, CyberRAG adopts an agentic design that enables dynamic control flow and adaptive reasoning. This architecture autonomously refines threat labels and natural-language justifications, reducing false positives and enhancing interpretability. It is also extensible: new attack types can be supported by adding classifiers without retraining the core agent. CyberRAG was evaluated on SQL Injection, XSS, and SSTI, achieving over 94% accuracy per class and a final classification accuracy of 94.92% through semantic orchestration. Generated explanations reached 0.94 in BERTScore and 4.9/5 in GPT-4-based expert evaluation, with robustness preserved against adversarial and unseen payloads. These results show that agentic, specialist-oriented RAG can combine high detection accuracy with trustworthy, SOC-ready prose, offering a flexible path toward partially automated cyber-defense workflows."

Nikitin et al. 2022 (AutoML, 159 words):

"The effectiveness of the machine learning methods for real-world tasks depends on the proper structure of the modeling pipeline. The proposed approach is aimed to automate the design of composite machine learning pipelines, which is equivalent to computation workflows that consist of models and data operations. The approach combines key ideas of both automated machine learning and workflow management systems. It designs the pipelines with a customizable graph-based structure, analyzes the obtained results, and reproduces them. The evolutionary approach is used for the flexible identification of pipeline structure. The additional algorithms for sensitivity analysis, atomization, and hyperparameter tuning are implemented to improve the effectiveness of the approach. Also, the software implementation on this approach is presented as an open-source framework. The set of experiments is conducted for the different datasets and tasks (classification, regression, time series forecasting). The obtained results confirm the correctness and effectiveness of the proposed approach in the comparison with the state-of-the-art competitors and baseline solutions."

Eccles et al. 2025, Mosaic (LLM systems, 182 words without the URL sentence):

"Extensive compute and memory requirements limit the deployment of large language models (LLMs) on any hardware. Compression methods, such as pruning, can reduce model size, which in turn reduces resource requirements. State-of-the-art pruning is based on coarse-grained methods. They are time-consuming and inherently remove critical model parameters, adversely impacting the quality of the pruned model. This paper introduces projection pruning, a novel fine-grained method for pruning LLMs. In addition, LLM projection pruning is enhanced by a new approach we refer to as composite projection pruning — the synergistic combination of unstructured pruning that retains accuracy and structured pruning that reduces model size. We develop Mosaic, a novel system to create and deploy pruned LLMs using composite projection pruning. Mosaic is evaluated using a range of performance and quality metrics on multiple hardware platforms, LLMs, and datasets. Mosaic is 7.19 × faster in producing models than existing approaches. Mosaic models achieve up to 84.2% lower perplexity and 31.4% higher accuracy than models obtained from coarse-grained pruning. Up to 67% faster inference and 68% lower GPU memory use is noted for Mosaic models."

Bruneel et al. 2026 (multi-agent LLM pipeline, 197 words):

"This publication presents an established engineering-driven framework for pipelining large language model (LLM) agents for automatic code translation. The framework is applied in a long-term ongoing industrial project with Danfoss Power Solutions aimed at translating five million lines of Delphi to C#. To manage this scale, the source codebase is divided into translatable chunks, which are processed independently through an agentic LLM pipeline, with subsequently the reassembly of the translated code chunks in the target language. Due to the inherent stochasticity of LLMs, the framework incorporates processing and validation functions to ensure consistency and correctness. As an extended version of our short paper (Bruneel et al., 2025), this paper provides a significantly more comprehensive architectural blueprint. Specifically, we present an in-depth background & related work section, formalise the chunking and reassembly strategies, detail the internal mechanics of agent stages, and introduce sequential agent orchestration alongside the dynamic LLM pipeline architecture. Furthermore, we report early empirical observations regarding the pipeline's computational overhead, validation dynamics, and translation quality, noting a practical impact of an initial 10x acceleration over estimated manual translation efforts. Ultimately, we share these expanded insights to inform future development and application in similar large-scale translation tasks."

Munley et al. 2024, LLM4VV (LLM evaluation, 205 words):

"Large language models (LLMs) are a new and powerful tool for a wide span of applications involving natural language and demonstrate impressive code generation abilities. The goal of this work is to automatically generate tests and use these tests to validate and verify compiler implementations of a directive-based parallel programming paradigm, OpenACC. To do so, in this paper, we explore the capabilities of state-of-the-art LLMs, including open-source LLMs - Meta's Codellama, Phind's fine-tuned version of Codellama, Deepseek's Deepseek Coder and closed-source LLMs - OpenAI's GPT-3.5-Turbo and GPT-4-Turbo. We further fine-tuned the open-source LLMs and GPT-3.5-Turbo using our own testsuite dataset along with using the OpenACC specification. We also explored these LLMs using various prompt engineering techniques that include code template, template with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), one-shot example, one-shot with RAG, expressive prompt with code template and RAG. This paper highlights our findings from over 5000 tests generated via all the above mentioned methods. Our contributions include: (a) exploring the capabilities of the latest and relevant LLMs for code generation, (b) investigating fine-tuning and prompt methods, and (c) analyzing the outcome of LLMs generated tests including manually analysis of representative set of tests. We found the LLM Deepseek-Coder-33b-Instruct produced the most passing tests followed by GPT-4-Turbo."

Bahmani, El Shawi et al. (arXiv precursor of FGCS 163, 2025; 192 words; the published abstract may differ):

"Novel technologies in automated machine learning ease the complexity of algorithm selection and hyperparameter optimization. Hyperparameters are important for machine learning models as they significantly influence the performance of machine learning models. Many optimization techniques have achieved notable success in hyperparameter tuning and surpassed the performance of human experts. However, depending on such techniques as black-box algorithms can leave machine learning practitioners without insight into the relative importance of different hyperparameters. In this paper, we consider building the relationship between the performance of the machine learning models and their hyperparameters to discover the trend and gain insights, with empirical results based on six classifiers and 200 datasets. Our results enable users to decide whether it is worth conducting a possibly time-consuming tuning strategy, to focus on the most important hyperparameters, and to choose adequate hyperparameter spaces for tuning. The results of our experiments show that gradient boosting and Adaboost outperform other classifiers across 200 problems. However, they need tuning to boost their performance. Overall, the results obtained from this study provide a quantitative basis to focus efforts toward guided automated hyperparameter optimization and contribute toward the development of better-automated machine learning frameworks."

Partial abstract, Van Landuyt et al. 2026 (benchmark of LLM tools; from a search snippet, wording close to the original): the study is "an experimental evaluation study of LLM-based threat elicitation tools applied to two complex application cases", with "four distinct baselines representative of results from human threat modelers, both novices and experts"; "56 distinct threat models generated by 6 LLM-based threat elicitation tools"; the generated threats are "somewhat similar to threats documented by human threat modelers", "relative performance is low", and "performance differences between these tools can be attributed on a similar level to both the prompting approach and the actual reasoning capabilities of the underlying LLMs being used."

What kinds of statements they make. Three recurring bullet types, usually in this order: (a) one problem or context statement ("LLMs are difficult to optimize without hardware and software accelerators."; "The current approaches lie far behind when predicting increases of consumption."); (b) two or three "what we built / what we did" statements, verb-first or noun-phrase ("Design a new JCT increase prediction model", "A novel low-cost derivative-free search strategy", "We consider 6 leading ..."); (c) one or two outcome statements with a number or a comparative ("Achieve up to 47.3× better", "up to 33% reduction in execution time", "outperforms SHAP in IIoT settings"). Comparison papers (N-body, smart contracts) write the finding itself as the bullet ("Portable programming models can achieve native performance."). Artefact availability is a common last bullet ("Free web API, web application and desktop system"; "Support for both local batch and SLURM-based").

## 4. Introduction anatomy

Length: 5–13 paragraphs (Kafka-ML 5, CyberRAG 7, Mosaic 7, PDD 7, ColabNAS 9, LLM4VV 9 incl. a "1.1. Motivation" subsection, Nikitin 13).

Paragraph roles, in order [pattern consistent across the 12 full texts]:
1. Broad context with one striking number ("GPT-3, a 175 billion parameter LLM, is over 350 GB in size"; "hundreds of thousands per hour"; "a forecast of 500 billion of connected devices by 2030").
2. Why the obvious solution falls short; "However" sentence. "However, most of the existing AutoML solutions are quite distanced from real business and industrial applications." (Nikitin). "Still, most RAG implementations retrieve context only once before generating output" (CyberRAG). "In short, existing pruning methods do not adequately balance runtime performance and LLM quality." (Mosaic).
3. Related approaches in one paragraph, citation-led ("[10] emphasized...", "[11] used...") in PDD; the gap sentence closes it ("There is a limited body of literature on ...").
4. One-sentence statement of aim: "The motivation of this article is to create small language models (SLMs) from foundation LLMs with similar quality while running on fewer resources." (Mosaic). "That is why we decided to develop a flexible approach that combines the main features of AutoML and WMS." (Nikitin).
5. Proposal paragraph: "To overcome the limitations of conventional RAG pipelines and the opacity of LLM-based agents in cybersecurity, we introduce CyberRAG, a modular and extensible agent-based RAG framework ..."; "For this reason, we present ColabNAS, an affordable HW NAS technique".
6. Contributions.
7. Outline.

Contribution statements. Present in 8 of 12 full texts (absent in Nikitin, ColabNAS, CyberRAG, Bahmani). Forms seen, verbatim lead-ins:

## 7. Ablations

- Naming: "5.4. Ablation Study: Impact of Retrieval" (CyberRAG) is the only headed ablation in the 12 full texts. Its whole text is four sentences: "To quantify the contribution of the retrieval module to the overall system performance, we conducted an ablation study by comparing the quality of explanations produced with and without the use of RAG. Each explanation was rated on a scale from 1 to 5 by an LLM-judge ... As seen in Figure 4, explanations produced with RAG consistently score higher across all models, with LLaMA3.1:8B reaching the highest mean rating of 4.9. This confirms the added value of retrieval for producing grounded and high-quality justifications." Presented as one grouped bar chart with series "With RAG" / "Without RAG" per model; the numeric ablation (84.75% without RAG vs 94.92% with) is reported in prose in 5.2.
- Elsewhere ablations are called by the component varied, not "ablation": Nikitin 4.3 compares "Simultaneous tuning", "Sequential tuning", "Serial isolated tuning"; Kafka-ML compares "Normal / Data streams / Data streams & containerization"; Mosaic puts "Calibration Sample Size", "Model Quantization", "Additional Calibration Datasets" in appendices D, F, G; a Highlights bullet of another FGCS paper reads "Comprehensive Ablation Analysis: Conducted detailed ablation studies on the reward function and state representation" (Reinforced model selection, FGCS 176).
- Variants as named rows or paired columns: Archetti lists "FedSurF", "FedSurF-C", "FedSurF-C-IPCW", "FedSurF-IBS", "FedSurF-AUC" as table rows with uniform sampling as the reference; Shafee uses paired columns "Original text" vs "Optimised text" as the prompt-refinement ablation and describes a key-token sweep in prose ("we conducted a series of smaller-scale experiments in which we varied the number of key tokens ... - Table layout: rows are named variants or methods ("Static (XGBoost)", "Composite (FEDOT)", "Variable (TPOT)", "Linear (MLBox)"; "Global / Layer / Projection"; "Unstructured / Composite / Structured"), columns are metrics or datasets; best value in bold ("Bold values denote the highest accuracy for each pruning target."; "The best results (↑) are highlighted in bold."), an asterisk for "not significantly different from the reference". ## 9. Statistical validation habits

Spread is wide; the modal habit in system and LLM papers is weak, the modal habit in benchmark papers is stronger.

- Single run, no variance, no test: CyberRAG (one 80/20 split), LLM4VV (one generation per prompt; "The output of LLMs is typically stochastic, so it cannot be guaranteed ..."), ColabNAS, Kafka-ML, PDD (one run per cell), Huang (one split, Accuracy/Precision/Recall/F1 table).
- Repeats with mean ± std or standard error: Nikitin "20 independent runs" for the benchmark tables and "Each experiment was repeated 100 times to analyze the both quality and stability of the tuning"; Mosaic "average from five trials, and one standard deviation"; Aslanpour "We repeat each experiment for a given rate at least three times to minimize statistical error and report the average as well as the standard error for each data point. Each experiment lasts for 10 min"; Shafee, for LLM-generated outputs: "the optimized evasion attack was repeated three times with fixed prompts and LLM parameters. Table 3 reports the mean FPR and standard deviation across three runs ... We additionally report the t-interval 95% confidence interval (CI) for each optimized experiment, to quantify the uncertainty induced by the stochastic nature of LLM-based text generation." (cells "75.0 ± 1.7 / [70.8, 79.2]"); Archetti "The reported results represent the mean and standard deviation computed over 20 runs." (cells "73.5 ± 0.7*").
- Tests: Bahmani uses the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for pairwise algorithm comparisons across 200 datasets, mean ranks with 95% CI, and a within-1% tie rule; Archetti uses "The Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by a pairwise Dunn's test at a significance level of 0.05 ... Any results not showing a statistically significant difference with the FedSurF-C performance are marked with an asterisk (*)." and justifies a design choice with a t-test over 90 pairings ("only a fraction (5.6%) displayed a statistically significant performance variation"); Shafee reports a Shapiro–Wilk normality test ("p = 3.2×10−6") and distribution distances (Wasserstein, KL); Zoppi's abstract advertises "a total of 23 public datasets" and "extensive experimentation, sensitivity analyses" (full text not accessible).
- Empirical upper and lower bounds as reference rows: Archetti evaluates every model in "three settings: Global, Local, and Federated", the global model being "an empirical upper bound" and the local average "an empirical lower bound".
- Not seen in the sample: Friedman test, Nemenyi post-hoc, critical difference diagrams, bootstrap CIs. [Inferred: these would not be out of place, but FGCS reviewers evidently accept mean ± std over 3–20 runs, a t-interval CI for stochastic LLM outputs, and a Wilcoxon, t-test or Kruskal–Wallis/Dunn when many datasets or pairings are involved.]
- Budgets are stated as the fairness device in AutoML comparisons: "The maximum time for evaluation was limited by 10 min for all approaches."; "a maximum number of generations ... 200; population size – 10 individuals." (Nikitin). Hardware is named ("NVIDIA A100 GPU", "Tesla T4 GPUs", "Perlmutter ... AMD EPYC CPUs and NVIDIA A100 GPUs", five platforms P1–P5 with a table).
- Held-out statements are explicit: "These data were never used during the training phase of either the classifiers or the RAG model." (CyberRAG).

## 10. Conclusion anatomy

Length: 130–670 words (Shafee 130, Bahmani 180, ColabNAS 230, Archetti 235, LLM4VV 260, CyberRAG 330, Mosaic 365, Kafka-ML 440, Nikitin 620, PDD 670). When a separate Discussion exists, the conclusion is the shorter of the two (CyberRAG 470 vs 330; LLM4VV 1100 vs 260; Shafee 1500 vs 130; Aslanpour's Discussion plus Conclusion total about 1080).

Paragraph roles [consistent]:
1. One sentence restating what was built or studied: "In this paper, Kafka-ML, an open-source framework to manage the pipeline of ML/AI applications through data streams has been presented."; "In this paper, we conducted six experiments across multiple real-life and synthetic datasets to compare the effectiveness of our proposed method, PDD, and the most commonly used concept drift detection methods".
2. Findings, usually qualitative; numbers restated in 3 of 8 (ColabNAS "in just 3.1 GPU hours ... 3.9 times less RAM, 31 times less Flash ... losing 2.8 per cent accuracy points on average"; CyberRAG restates "from 84.75% to 94.92%" in Discussion; LLM4VV "81.4% passing tests (1087/1335) while the highest passing rate from Stage 2 using Deepseek is 48% (170/351)" in Discussion). Mosaic restates nothing numeric: "Mosaic produces faster models than existing methods. Mosaic models have lower perplexity and higher accuracy than coarse-grained pruning."
3. Limitations as one paragraph, no heading: "One of the main limitations of this approach is its dependence on the quality of the data within the knowledge base and the precision of the classifier." (CyberRAG); "The PDD method has some disadvantages, such as computational cost and infeasible use for multi-class classification." (PDD); "We evaluate our approach only for a limited set of benchmarks and do not claim to have the superior approach for all tasks." (Nikitin); "The harsh truth about the current state of LLMs is that they can generate false and misleading output at times ... We emphasize the need to exercise caution and conduct human evaluation on all outputs that are used in production." (LLM4VV).
   Archetti's conclusion is a model for a comparison paper with a mixed result: "The algorithm outperforms local models and achieves performance metrics comparable to global models"; "our findings reveal that any sampling strategy, except for uniform sampling, yields results close to the best."; "While DeepSurv exhibits strong performance, its training procedure requires iterative averaging of model parameters, leading to heavy bandwidth usage. In contrast, FedSurF++ demands only a single tree exchange round"; closing "In conclusion, the FedSurF++ algorithm proves to be a valuable alternative to neural-network-based models ... as it achieves comparable performance while preserving data privacy and offering an efficient solution in terms of communication."
4. Future work, one paragraph, an enumerated list, or a bold-lead bullet list. Aslanpour: "Future research can be conducted to extend those insights in the following aspects: (a) self-adaptive load balancing policies for variable objectives, (b) ... and (h), application-specific evaluation of AI models for load balancing." Other forms: "As future work, we have pointed out the following challenges and improvements to Kafka-ML: • Distributed inference. ... • Distributed training. Currently, training is performed in a single container that may not be enough for large neural networks." (Kafka-ML); "Looking ahead, future developments will aim to expand the scope of supported attack types" (CyberRAG); "Applying projection pruning to Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, such as Mixtral or DeepSeek, presents a promising direction for future research." (Mosaic); "One future direction is to study the hyperparameters for different machine learning problems, including regression and clustering algorithms." (Bahmani).
5. Optional closing sentence of significance (CyberRAG: "Overall, CyberRAG emerges as a robust, innovative, and forward-looking prototype"; ColabNAS: a sustainability sentence). Several papers skip it.
