\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Automated machine learning has been reshaped by large language models. Systems
built on them plan experiments, write and repair code, read execution logs, and
interpret their own results, capabilities that classical AutoML frameworks,
which search a fixed space of pipelines, never had. The design space of such
systems is now large and largely uncharted: implementations differ in how much
autonomy they grant the model, how they inject knowledge about libraries and
methods, and how tightly they constrain the sequence of actions.

What has not been measured is how much any of this is worth. Papers in this area
typically introduce one system and report that it improves on a previous one,
with the backbone model, the scaffold, and the evaluation protocol all changing
at once. Two consequences follow. Comparisons across papers cannot separate the
contribution of the architecture from that of the model, and, more
uncomfortably, agentic systems are rarely measured against the baseline a
competent practitioner would reach for. Where a tuned gradient boosting model
appears at all, it usually appears untuned.

This paper isolates the architectural axis. We fix the backbone to a single open
31B-parameter model and vary only the surrounding system across three points of
the design space:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{FEDOT.LLM}, a rigid pipeline in which the model interprets the
        task and configures a fixed AutoML backend, with code generated from
        templates that carry fixed input/output contracts;
  \item \textbf{AutoDS-Tools}, a multi-agent system of six specialised agents
        communicating through structured reports, with a shared toolkit and
        broad freedom of action;
  \item \textbf{Terminus-2}, an open harness that gives the model a shell, a
        working directory and an execution loop, and prescribes nothing else.
\end{itemize}

All three are executed inside a common environment that records every attempt,
error, token and unit of cost, and all are evaluated on the official temporal
splits of TabReD~\citep{rubachev2024tabred}, a benchmark of real-world tabular
tasks with distribution shift, against the eighteen expert-tuned reference
methods published with it. Because those references are Optuna-tuned and
averaged over fifteen seeds, they constitute a ceiling that reflects competent
practice rather than a default configuration.

\paragraph{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A measurement of the ceiling.} Under an identical backbone, the
        architectures span $0.26$ to $0.85$ of the range defined by the published
        baselines, while tuned XGBoost sits at $0.87$ and the best published
        method at $0.95$. The strongest architecture we tested is level with
        tuned LightGBM at $0.85$ and ahead of tuned CatBoost at $0.80$, but does
        not close the gap to XGBoost, from which it is $0.02$ short. We state this as a boundary rather than a defeat: it
        delimits what scaffolding can and cannot buy at a given model scale.
  \item \textbf{A non-monotonic relationship, and a mechanism for it.}
        Performance does not increase with architectural weight, and it does not
        decrease with it either. The most constrained system ranks near the
        bottom of the leaderboard, and the highest-ranking of the three is
        neither extreme. We argue that constraint pays only where it removes a
        decision the weak model reliably gets wrong, and costs elsewhere, and we
        give direct evidence from an ablation whose sign flips between task
        families---though the two directions carry very unequal weight, the
        cost being total on three tasks of eight and the benefit defensible on
        one. Both arms that separate architecture from
        prescription and provisioning have now been run: provisioning is worth
        nothing, prescription is worth three times the architectural gap between
        the two agentic systems, and the ordering survives both
        (Section~\ref{sec:ceiling}).
  \item \textbf{A separation of two axes usually conflated.} What the literature
        treats as one ``knowledge injection'' intervention is at least two:
        prescribing which class of tool to use (\Ktool) and prescribing how to
        train and validate (\Kdisc). We show by direct measurement of the
        instruction layer that the tool prescription is between $9.1\%$ and
        $26.0\%$ of the text it gives its name to, that the two halves are
        sub-additive rather than complementary on either aggregation we can
        form---and on the normalised scale the tool half alone outscores both
        halves together---and, by reading the traces of the
        system that receives no prescription, that on tabular data the tool
        half prescribes what the model already chooses unaided. Together these
        offer an explanation for the inconsistent effects reported for aggregate
        knowledge and memory ablations.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Relation to the neutral-scaffold assumption.}
Terminal-Bench 2.0 introduces Terminus~2 as a deliberately minimal scaffold, a
single tool and a headless terminal, built to mitigate the biases of richer agent
scaffolds so that a leaderboard over it reads as a comparison between
\emph{models}~\citep{merrill2026terminalbench}. That reading is only sound if
the scaffold contributes little. It is already under pressure in the coding
domain, where holding the model fixed and swapping the harness moves aggregate
score by more than twenty points~\citep{harnessbench2026} and tokens per solved
task by up to a factor of forty~\citep{scaffoldeffect2026}. We ask the question
where a strong non-agentic reference exists and can calibrate the answer: with
the backbone held constant, the three systems as they ship span $0.59$ of the
range defined by eighteen expert-tuned baselines, and $0.49$ of it once the
prescribed-knowledge layer is removed from the system that carries one, so that
what remains is architecture alone. Holding the architecture fixed and swapping
the backbone instead, across an elevenfold spread in price, moves it by at most
$0.24$ --- and that larger figure rests
on a single task, without which it is $0.07$. A scaffold is never neutral; on this domain it is the dominant
term of the two.
