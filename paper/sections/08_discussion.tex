\subsection{What the results say about harness design}

On TabReD, AutoDS-Tools with its instruction file ranks first, and
FEDOT.LLM and Terminus-2 are level on average. The order follows who builds
the model and whether it is tuned. An agent writing its own code fits one
untuned gradient-boosting model. LightAutoML tunes and blends its boosting
models. The FEDOT backend searches only when fitting its default pipeline
leaves time, which happened on two TabReD tasks of eight. With the model
built the same way, the harness changes the mean score little. It decides
reliability, wall-clock time, the tasks a system can attempt and whether a
library call ends in a submission. FEDOT.LLM calls its backend on every
task, and the repair loop of AutoDS-Tools waits out the long LightAutoML fit
(no cell lost more than one trial of 24). Terminus-2 with the same
instruction submits nothing in \ktToolLost{} of \ktToolTrials{} trials,
because it reads the silent terminal of a long fit as a failure.

Task by task, AutoDS-Tools with its file is the best of the three on six of
eight TabReD tasks. Its gain from the file grows with the distance between
the hand-written model and tuned gradient boosting, from near zero on
\texttt{homesite-insurance} and \texttt{delivery-eta} to the largest on
\texttt{ecom-offers} and \texttt{cooking-time}; on
\texttt{sberbank-housing}, the smallest table, LightAutoML adds nothing.
FEDOT.LLM wins on classification, where its default ensemble is strong
(\texttt{ecom-offers}, F-DATA), and on the small \texttt{sberbank-housing},
where its search runs; on large regression tables it trails with an
untuned random forest. Terminus-2 wins no TabReD task. On maize, whose test
set holds unseen location-years, its simple models transfer better than
the tuned ensemble of AutoDS-Tools.

The file helps in the setting it was written for: its own harness, a task
solved from scratch, the task family of the library it names. Appended to
the Terminus-2 task, it leaves \ktBothLost{} of 24 to \ktToolLost{} of
\ktToolTrials{} trials per cell without a submission; given a working
script, it leaves \mlabWiped{} of \mlabRepeated{} MLAgentBench tasks without
one. Both failures come from specific sentences, the advice on training
time and the instruction to replace the supplied tool, and switching the
whole file on and off cannot locate them. This may explain why published
ablations of instruction files and retrieved knowledge disagree: a file
measured in one harness and from one starting point has to be measured
again in another.

No configuration reaches the strongest published method; the best cell is
on par with tuned XGBoost. The reference methods are tuned over fifteen
seeds, and the traces point to the next addition to test: a hyperparameter
search that the agent runs over its own code, which none of our agents
attempted.

\subsection{Guidance for practitioners}

The results give one rule per situation. When a strong tuned baseline
exists, use it: the best cell in the paper is on par with tuned XGBoost and
every cell is below the strongest published method. For a tabular task from
scratch with accuracy first and an hour per task acceptable, use
AutoDS-Tools with its instruction file, which hands the modelling to an
AutoML library. For a first pass over a new scientific dataset in minutes
per attempt, use Terminus-2 with the task text alone; it beat the author
baseline on maize, exceeded the author figure on F-DATA and found the
published featurisation on OpenPoly. Use FEDOT.LLM for classification, where
its default ensemble made it the best of the three on \texttt{ecom-offers}
and F-DATA, and for tables small enough for its search to run. On large
regression tables its default random forest ranks near the bottom. When
a working script is supplied, prescribe nothing. Two rules hold across at
least two protocols: prescribe only the decision the model would otherwise
get wrong, and keep advice about training time out of the prompt of any
harness with a hard time limit.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:threats}

\emph{Repeats.} Published baselines are means over fifteen seeds; agent rows
are means of three attempts. With eight tasks the paired tests have little
power, and we report them alongside win counts and intervals.

\emph{The repair loop.} AutoDS-Tools repairs failed executions more often
with the file than without (Section~\ref{sec:friction}), so it partially
masks the cost of the file. We report the counts without adjustment; an
experiment that disables the loop in both branches would isolate this
effect.

\emph{The lost trial.} One of the 24 AutoDS-Tools trials on TabReD
reached the one-hour limit with nothing to score and is excluded, so
\texttt{homesite-insurance} rests on two attempts. Scoring that attempt at
the weakest published value would move the normalised mean of AutoDS-Tools
from $\nAutoDS$ to $\sensLostNorm$, still ahead of the other two
harnesses.

\emph{Data access.} Several MLAgentBench tasks use subsets of the original
data and none an official test split, so every comparison there is
internal. On TabReD the agents receive the labelled validation split
(Section~\ref{sec:benchmarks}), a small advantage that works against our
main conclusion.

\emph{The backbone.} The backbone ablation rests on three models, two from
one vendor, and one task family. A stronger model that reasons less
verbosely might not push the multi-agent system into the time limit, and
our runs cannot separate capability from verbosity.

\emph{Generality of the harnesses.} The three systems are one instance each
of a workflow, a multi-agent system and a minimal harness. A different
six-agent system or a different fixed-stage workflow could behave
differently. The method of separating harness, instruction file and
backbone carries over to other systems; the numbers describe these three.
