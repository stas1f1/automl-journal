\section{What the execution traces show}
\label{sec:mechanism}

% Remaster, 6 October 2026.

Harbor records every trial's execution trace. We read the traces for three
things: who builds the model, whether the discipline instruction changes the
agent's behaviour, and how often an instruction sends the agent into repair.

\subsection{Who builds the model: the agent or an AutoML library}
\label{sec:spontaneous}

Without a library instruction, both agents build the model themselves.
Terminus-2 fitted a gradient-boosting model on every TabReD task in every
attempt: LightGBM in 23 of the 24 trials of Section~\ref{sec:abl-image} and
XGBoost in the remaining one; on the stock image it installed LightGBM
itself on six of the eight tasks. AutoDS-Tools without the library
instruction wrote LightGBM, XGBoost or CatBoost by hand in all 24 trials of
each of its two cells without it. The configuration is hand-written and
near-identical across tasks and attempts: for Terminus-2, 1\,000 estimators
in all 24 trials, a learning rate of $0.05$ in 23, early stopping on a
holdout in 23. No trace of either agent contains a hyperparameter search,
by Optuna, grid, randomised search or a cross-validated sweep.

With the library instruction, AutoDS-Tools calls LightAutoML in all 24
trials of each cell that carries it. LightAutoML at its defaults fits a
linear model, LightGBM and CatBoost, tunes the two boosting models with
Optuna within its time budget and blends the results; a rerun of the
prescribed call on F-DATA recorded 101 Optuna trials per boosting model. The
library instruction therefore replaces one hand-written model with an AutoML
search, and this is the change that lifts AutoDS-Tools to the level of
tuned XGBoost (Section~\ref{sec:abl-file}). No configuration reaches the
strongest published method, an MLP-PLR ensemble that the benchmark tuned
over fifteen seeds. The per-trial extraction of
learners and settings is released with the paper.

\subsection{The discipline instruction reaches the agent and leaves the score unchanged}
\label{sec:discipline}

The discipline instruction moves the task metric by $\gridDiscMeanU\%$ on
average (Table~\ref{tab:grid}), and the traces show that the agent follows
it. Three clauses ask it to compare against a trivial predictor, to plan the
time budget before starting, and to keep the variant that validates best.
All three behaviours appear in every trial of the two cells with the
discipline instruction and in no trial of the two cells without it. On
these tabular tasks the agent's own choices were already adequate, so
following the instruction changed little in the score.

\subsection{Instructions make execution fail more often}
\label{sec:friction}

AutoDS-Tools contains a repair loop, invoked when execution fails. The loop
was not logged in the repeated runs, so the count comes from an earlier pair
of runs (all eighteen TabReD and MLAgentBench tasks once per cell, on the
earlier data preparation of Section~\ref{sec:coverage}), and we report only
the direction. The loop fired 18 times with the file and 12 times without
it, mostly on tasks for which the file names a specialised library: four
invocations against none on \texttt{clrs} and three against none on
\texttt{amp-parkinsons}.

The largest loss on MLAgentBench traces back to one clause. The discipline
instruction tells the agent not to stop after ``an arbitrary small budget (5
or 15 epochs is almost always badly undertrained)'' and offers
``$\sim 100$ epochs'' as a starting point. On \texttt{clrs} the agent
without the file chose 5 to 30 epochs and used 92 of a 120-minute budget on
one attempt. With the file it attempted a hundred epochs and made no
submission in three attempts. Instructions can therefore increase execution
failures, and their net effect is positive only when the gain from the
prescribed tool offsets those failures.
